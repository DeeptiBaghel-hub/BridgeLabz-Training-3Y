The Weekly Read

In the early days of print journalism, a page's column count was dictated purely by the width of a printing press. Today it survives as a design choice rather than a necessity, prized for how it shortens line length and keeps long-form text readable without forcing the eye to travel too far left to right.

Modern readers encounter multi-column layouts less often on the web than in print, largely because scrolling behavior does not map cleanly onto columns the way flipping a physical page does. Even so, the effect remains a favorite for print-style feature articles, museum placards, and printable newsletters.

Designers who use the CSS column-count property must account for images, headings, and pull quotes that might otherwise split awkwardly across a column break. The column-span property solves part of that problem by letting an element ignore the column structure entirely.

Justified text, long associated with newspapers, adds to the illusion of a printed page, though it requires careful attention to word spacing so that gaps between words don't become distracting on narrower columns.

Ultimately, the column layout endures because it slows readers down in a good way: it signals that the content in front of them deserves the kind of sustained attention a printed page once commanded.
